AI Paranoia

21 May 2026 Draft
AI Thoughts

I now live in a constant state of AI-induced paranoia. Every time I read an article on the internet or watch a YouTube video I’m constantly trying to figure out if it was written by AI or not. I look out for the infamous tells of negative parallelism (“it’s not this, it’s that”), a surplus of m-dashes, and other numerous verbal ticks like “Honestly”, “Here’s the kicker” and “Delve into”.

While I’ve gotten pretty good at telling AI writing, it is much more difficult for me to tell AI-speak when a human is narrating it, like in a YouTube video. Multiple times I’ve had to rewind a video to try to figure out if the last thing the person said was AI-generated. I can’t even have a relaxing meal with a YouTube video anymore. Multiple times I turned off a video half-way through in a silent rage because the script sounded AI-generated.

Researching basic information has become impossible because the top links in browsers are now either straight up AI generated websites with hilariously poorly generated “authors” or legitimate websites from real companies that host an article that seems suspicious. It’s not difficult to imagine somebody in their marketing department using AI to churn out an article for promotion. If I get even a wiff of AI, I can no longer trust the information on that site, especially if it’s for something important, like work.

I can no longer simply find a playlist on youtube to play while doing the dishes. I have to first assess the thumbnail, the channel, and read the comments to assess whether it is AI music or not. One time I unwittingly listened to a playlist of classic French tunes. I knew what the songs are supposed to sound like, which is why I was taken aback when some of them just sounded… off. The instrumentals were different and the artist did not sound right. I thought it might be a cover artist, so I looked for the track list. There was no tracklist in the description box. There was a tracklist on the thumbnail though, but it did not match the list of songs that were actually playing. The comments looked like they were all bots and boomers. As I was looking at the tracklist I noticed the photo of a famous French artist on it. Something about him looked off. Was this even a real photo? I reverse image searched and found that it was, but it seemed slightly edited. Smoothed over, his wrinkles were less prominent and it seemed higher resolution. That’s when I realized I just listened to AI generarted music for fifteen minutes straight. I was so angry with myself, I turned my phone off and finished the dishes in silence.

The AI enshittification of the Internet has led to a total annihilation of the little trust that I already had in the Internet. I have developed as a result a sort of hypervigilance about the content I consume online. But what is it about AI content that is so repulsive? James Koebler, in his article titled “…” writes that the AI writing style is bland and often non-sensical, like a jumble of corporate buzzwords. It seems to lack any personality. It is too polished, too perfect, too safe. Totally inhuman. Clearly I’m not the only person instinctively repulsed by it. But is it really the style of AI writing that I, as many others, find so unpleasant? AI is improving at lightspeed (do I need to bring up Will Smith eating spaghetti again?), and it is very likely that in a few years, its voice will be indistinguishable from that of a humans. This rejection goes deeper than the uncanny valley. Every time I encouter AI-generated content online I see it as one thing only – deception.

Why would anybody use AI to create online content? The better question is, why do people create online content in the first place. In the modern internet the answer is simple. It’s mooney. Everything can now be monitized, from a listicle website to a TikTok account to a Substack. More and more people are becoming aware that there is easy money to be made on the internet, simply by going viral. A nurse was able to quit her dayjob because the Instagram account she made for her Golden Retreiver was bringing in $20,000 a month. Many other people, working difficult real-world jobs, not paid nearly enough, see that by going viral on social media can pay them more in a month than they make in a year. As a result, there is a mass of people who want to carve a piece of the internet fame pie for themeselves at any cost. And the one thing the internet rewards more than anything else, is quantity. [the reign of quantitty?]

The most common advice for starting a YouTube channel is post consistently. Post on a schedule. Find a niche and only post content in that niche. The algorithm rewards quantity and consistency; the people know it and now they have access to a tool that can output mass quantities of content in no time at a relatively low cost. People use AI to put content online for one reason and one reason only – to make money, be it from google ads, sponsorhips, commissions, or direct sales. And this is what feels so gross about generated content. It’s the person behind it, simply using a tool to churn out as much slop as possible to make as much money as possible with as little effort as possible.

The best example of this is So and So – a medical student who made thousands of dollars off of an AI-generate MAGA influencer. He said that he chose to make here MAGA because MAGA people are dumb and will fall for it. And he was right. He was bringing in X dollars a month before Instagram shut down his account. So And So’s use of AI was a cynical cash grab on stupid people who cannot tell the difference or those who can but do not care. But he is not the only one.

There are multitudes of people giving advice on how to start a business online using AI. One popular business idea is drop-shipping t-shirts, mugs, and other items with printed AI-generated desings on them. They made Etsy practically unusable now. The first page of results for any query on that website is now filled with AI-enhanced products. This creates an extra mental load of having to sift through them to find something that was made by a real artist, and when you find that something that appears real, you have to scrutinize it to be sure that it is in fact real. While Etsy has tried to take steps to combat AI slop on their site, it is futile, and it’s probably a performative gesture anyway, since their business model rests on taking commission from sales. They are only happy if more sellers are enabled by AI to sell their wares on their site.

I’m not the first person to write about AI slop flooding the internet, or enshittfication in general. The usual tech enthusiast take focuses on the technology itself – calling it names like a fancy auto-complete, or a large matrix multiplication etc. They tend to focus on the technology itself, seemingly abstracting the blame away from the people who type prompts and run the agents. I am the opposite. I don’t hate AI – it’s just a tool. My problem is not with AI – my problem is with the people who use it. AI has enabled the people who were previously not technologically savvy enough or simply did not have enough time to be content creators, to also get a chance of going viral. In a way, it leveled the playing field, or busted open the gates of a gated community. It turned the internet into the equivalent of a meat market where people are hawking crap left and right, all claiming to have the best price as their meat rots in the heat.

Why are we seeing this mass influx of AI-powered merchants? It is simply because there is an incentive. Numerous stories of viral internet successes promise easy money to be made, and quantity is rewarded over quality. Many are struggling financially and see it as a chance to make some extra income. It’s hard to blame these people – if easy money is to be made, why not do it? Especially when the process can be automated with AI. While it may seem easy though, most will still fail because it still requires a lot of work. These users manage to generate a bunch of AI content and pollute the internet with it before their business fails. Imagine if every time a brick and mortar restaraunt went out of business, which happens to over 80% of them, the owner simply abandoned the building and let it stand there, dirty dishes still in the sink and all, without anybody being able to take it down, ruining the view of the shopping street while new restraunts try to work next to it.

But nobody is happy about this. Numerous internet users complain that the internet is not the same internet they remember growing up: now it is full of bots, extremely commercialized, everything is an ad, everything is a sponsorship, everybody is trying to sell you something. It is not the nerd plaground where people used to go because they wanted to, because it was fun, not because it’s one of their three jobs. And yet, few seem to want to address the root cause of the issue, the economic inctentives. It is much easier to discuss AI in the abstract, make fun of its stunted writing style and lament the loss of the internet of yesteryear. The only way to de-shittify the internet is to remove this incentive, but doing so is a lot harder. I only see two possible ways out of this.

The first way is a sort of digital social Darwinism – just let natural selection do its work. As the internet becomes more flooded with AI slop, eventually casual internet users, the primary customers of the AI content creators, will be turned off, as it will become unbearable to use the internet. They will frequent the big money-making social media platforms less, will perhaps turn to smaller, more human centric niche platforms like the Fediverse in response to being bombarded by AI-generated videos of obese women jumping on glass bridges. As traffic plummets, the money is no longer easy to make and most AI content creators will fail and try something else. This sounds nice, because we don’t have to do anything. The body will kill the infection with a feaver and everything will go back to normal. This is also unrealistic. As AI gets better, soon enough AI content will be indistinguishable from human content, and an a casual internet user will not be able to tell the two apart. They will see simply an abundance of new content every day, and will be more than happy. Relying on the descernment of the average internet user is the blind leading the blind. Algorithms will also likely learn to prioritize AI content and push down human made content, so it will be more difficult for any human-oriented online communities to form. If left unchecked, people will never reject AI, like a marble circling a bowl – gravity always pulls down.

From this, one may draw the logical conclusion that we should try to ban AI content. Filter it, restrict it, rank it lower in the algorithm, do anything to make it harder to make money with AI to disincentivize it. But this plan is even less realistic than the first. Moderation of internet content on that scale is impossible, and even if it was, who would do it? Companies on whose platforms the content is being posted on? Never, because they take a cut of the generated revenue. Would it be indivisual users, who would install AI-block the way they installed adblock? You would be schocked to learn that only 30% of internet users use adblock, which is the reason advertisements is a viable source of income on the internet. They wouldn’t block AI either. And that’s why blocking it is never going to work.

The only real solution is to alleviate the economic problems that are causing people to turn to the internet as a source of secondary income. A man went viral on TikTok for showing clips from his everyday life as an office worker. Him making coffee in the morning, driving to work, sitting in the office, going to the gym, etc. He began making a lot more money off his TikTok account than at his day job. Eventually, he quit his dayjob to do TikTok full time, but his TikTok popularity hinged on him showing his vignettes from his dayjob. He was eventually caught renting out a fake office space to pretend like he continued going to the office. Needless to say, he lost most of his following, and I he probably had to go back to an office job. None of this would have happened if him and others like him did not need a second income. However, I’m not an economist and do not have any solutions to the economic crisis that is leading so many trying their luck selling AI content online.

None of the above are realistic solutions to the AI problem on a large scale. AI content is here to stay. Already last year, more text on the internet was written by AI than by humans, so there is no escape. But I reject doom and gloom. I believe in making small positive changes in everydady life to make life better for yourself an others. So do your best to not engage with AI content, because the only reason it exists is engagement for somebody to make money off of it. Do not give sloppers your views, likes or comments, and especially money. On the internet, we do not vote with our feet but with our eyes. Refuse to spend time looking at AI content to prevent the creators from earning money off of it. It might lead some of them to quit.

I do however want to make it clear that I do not hate people who use AI to churn out content, in fact, I feel a lot of sympathy for them. They are likely doing it because they need the extra cash, and that’s tough. Those who are genuinely desperate and need anything, I sympathize with them, and if they manage to sell an AI-generated design on a mug and make enough to pay a bill, that’s fine. Even before AI there were people trying to make money on the internet. I also do not blame your people who see that jobs like teacher, nurse, and farmer pay peanuts for long difficult hours whereas tiktok celebrities make thousands of dollars a day from their living rooms, and want to do the same. They are simply learning from the adults around them. All of these people are trying to survive in a difficult time and AI became their weapon of choice.

With all this being said, I do still see hope for the internet. It will be found in small human-centered communities of active users, not passive consumers of content. Going on the internet will in a way become the thing it used to be back in the days of family computers. A deliberate activity of exploration. The AI-infused commercial layer of the web will likely not die, but will continue to prosper, because most people never wanted to “go online” in the first place. They open an app and scroll while waiting for their food to microwave. Something tells me they will be OK with AI content, especially when it gets better over time. Others, seeking a place where they don’t have to scruitinize every piece of content for LLM signatures in a state of AI-induced paranoia, will find their place.
